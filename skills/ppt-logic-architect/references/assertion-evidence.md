# Assertion-Evidence Framework

Each content slide makes **one assertion** as a full-sentence headline and **proves it with evidence** on the slide. Associated with Michael Alley's research on scientific presentation design. This skill uses it at the architecture level: what the headline claims and what evidence supports it. Visual layout is out of scope.

## 1. The rule

| Instead of a topic label | Write an assertion |
|---|---|
| "User Research" | "Eight of twelve users avoided the heavy knife because it hurt their wrists" |
| "Battery Results" | "Adaptive sampling cut energy use 34% in bench tests with the lab's activity mix" |
| "Market Overview" | "Refill purchases now outsell new units in the three largest regions" |

A headline is a **claim**: subject, verb, outcome. The body of the slide is the **evidence** for that claim.

## 2. Three things to keep apart

| Element | Role | Example |
|---|---|---|
| **Claim** (headline) | What the slide asserts | "Walking accuracy stays within tolerance" |
| **Data** (evidence) | What was observed or measured | "MAE 2.3 bpm, n=8, treadmill walk, 10 min" |
| **Interpretation** (explanation) | What the data means, and why | "The motion artifact is small at walking cadence" |

Label which is which. A slide that shows only data has no claim; a slide that shows only a claim has no proof; a slide that blends interpretation into "data" overstates what was measured.

## 3. Headline tests

1. **Disagree test**: could a reasonable audience member disagree? If not, it is a label or a truism.
2. **Proof test**: does the evidence on the slide prove *this* claim, and *no more*?
3. **Quantifier test**: do "all", "most", "reduces", "proves", "causes" match what was shown? Prefer the narrow claim that the evidence earns: "reduced by 34% in bench tests" rather than "reduces by 34%".
4. **Headline-chain test**: read only the headlines in order. They should tell the story as a paragraph, with no jumps.
5. **One-claim test**: if a headline joins two claims with "and", consider splitting the slide.

## 4. Slides that are not assertions

Title, agenda, section divider, summary, appendix, and Q&A slides may use labels. Each still states its purpose and relation to its neighbours in the card. A divider's real job is to restate the section claim, so write it as the claim, not the topic name.

## 5. Matching evidence to claim type

Suggest a visual *form*, not a layout:

| The claim is about | Evidence form |
|---|---|
| A difference between conditions | Comparison chart with baseline and sample size |
| Change over time | Trend plot with the relevant window |
| A relationship | Scatter or paired plot, with the caveat that it is not causal unless shown |
| Parts of a whole | Breakdown with defined whole |
| How something works or proceeds | Flow, diagram, or annotated schematic |
| A physical form or detail | Annotated photo or render with callouts |
| What users said or did | Quotes with participant counts and the coding basis |
| A decision between options | Criteria matrix with the criteria derived from requirements |

For qualitative evidence, include `n` and what the quotes are chosen from. Do not present one quote as a finding.

## 6. Pitfalls

- **Topic-label headlines** in a deck that otherwise claims things.
- **Headline stronger than the slide's evidence.**
- **Evidence that supports a different claim** from the headline.
- **Two claims, one slide**, so one goes unproven.
- **A claim repeated across slides** in different words.
- **Decoration standing in for evidence.**

## 7. Card mapping

Headline = *Action-oriented Headline*. The data and interpretation go in *Supporting Evidence*. Anything the headline claims that the evidence does not show goes into *Evidence Source / Confidence* as a gap.
